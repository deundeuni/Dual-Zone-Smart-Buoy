This idea white paper proposes the concept of a Dual-Zone Smart Buoy, expanding the role of marine buoys from mere navigational aids (survival coordinates) to ecological habitats (ecosystem coordinates). Divided by the waterline, the Top Zone utilizes a mushroom microstructure surface and adheres to IALA standards to mitigate barnacle fouling for navigation safety. Conversely, the Bottom Zone incorporates artificial reef surface treatments to intentionally encourage the settlement of barnacles, shellfish, and seaweed, forming a 5-tier vertical ecosystem. Furthermore, the Top Zone features a Charging-Reflection-Identification triple-function surface and defines an Amber-based EPS mode as an emergency power system to mitigate light pollution for the bottom ecosystem. The revised edition additionally records, as a direction for future exploration, a small station-keeping floating body ("Seed Boat") as an application form of Matrix 4. This paper aims not to claim legal exclusivity, but to offer a direction for future sustainable marine infrastructure that coexists with ocean ecosystems.

## 1. Problem Statement: Limitations of Styrofoam Buoys and Single-Purpose Coordinates

- **Environmental Limitations and Regulatory Trends of Conventional Buoys —** Most existing buoys installed at sea are made of styrofoam (expanded polystyrene) or simple polymers, breaking down into microplastics under wave action and sunlight. Consequently, environmental regulations are tightening globally. For instance, the Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries of South Korea prohibits new installations of expanded polystyrene buoys in aquaculture farms and actively promotes eco-friendly alternatives. Furthermore, toxic anti-fouling paints used to prevent biofouling are emerging as targets of international marine pollution regulations.
- **Human-Centric Single-Purpose Nature —** The role of traditional buoys is restricted strictly to "survival coordinates" for human utility, such as navigational aids, territorial boundary markers, and vessel safety, acting as discarded waste or physical obstacles to marine organisms.
- **Risks of Abandonment —** Abandoned buoys and mooring structures lacking management entities are difficult to recover, leading to persistent microplastic fragmentation. If moorings snap, adrift buoys endanger vessel navigation. Abandoned lines and nets induce "ghost fishing," entangling marine life, while additives in foam materials or heavy metals in anti-fouling paints gradually leach out. Ghost fishing and abandoned gear are documented in FAO/UNEP and OECD reports; the distribution and impacts of styrofoam buoys in Korean aquaculture are covered in J. Mar. Sci. Eng. (2024); the release of flame retardants (HBCD) from expanded polystyrene into seawater is addressed by KIOST researchers; and the marine impacts of anti-fouling paint particles and biocides are studied by Plymouth University, among others (see Section 8, verified at title/abstract level). Quantitative risk magnitudes are not verified in this white paper. Because the concepts in this paper could pose similar risks if abandoned, recovery obligations, identification markers, and detachable recovery structures under Section 4.4 are assumed as prerequisites.
- **Problem Statement —** Can artificial structures such as buoys fulfill their navigational safety function for human mariners while simultaneously serving as "ecosystem coordinates" that provide safe habitat spaces for marine life?

## 2. Core Concept: Waterline-Based Dual-Zone Structure and Biomimetic Spectrum

The physical surface characteristics and functional purposes are completely dualized along the boundary of the waterline.

### Top Zone (Above Waterline: Anti-fouling Orientation)

- **Triple-Function Surface (Charging-Reflection-Identification) —** Employs a Smooth Mushroom Microstructure to reduce the physical adhesion force of barnacle cyprid larvae (Barnacle Archetype) and mitigate surface fouling. Based on this, it aims for passive location identification via solar charging and retroreflection during the day, switching to active powered location identification via an Emergency Power System (EPS) at night.
- **Identification and Standards —** Designed to comply with IALA (International Association of Marine Aids to Navigation and Lighthouse Authorities) standards, equipping visual paint (red/yellow), a radar reflector for vessel radar detection, and an LED night beacon fitted with a full-cutoff shield to prevent downward light spillage.
- **Purpose —** Fulfills the survival coordinate function by maintaining a clean, lightweight surface state to provide clear positional information to fishing vessels and mariners.

### Bottom Zone (Below Waterline: Artificial Reef & Dynamic Ballast Orientation / Independent Auxiliary Entity)

- **Surface Characteristics —** Incorporates surface treatments with micro-grooves and pores to facilitate the settlement of barnacles, shellfish, and seaweed spores.
- **Identity as an Independent Auxiliary Entity —** The Bottom Zone is treated not as a subordinate structure permanently attached to the buoy body, but as an independent ecosystem auxiliary module that can be attached, detached, or replaced via Magnetic/Mechanical Quick-Release Clamping and mooring chains. This secures operational flexibility for horizontal transplanting, harvesting, cleaning, and module swapping.
- **Ecological and Structural Functions —** Intentionally induces biological settlement to convert the lower buoy section into a small artificial reef, while utilizing the weight of attached organisms as natural lower ballast (Self-Ballasting) to lower the overall center of gravity.
- **Purpose —** Serves as ecosystem coordinates providing habitats for small crustaceans, juvenile fish, and seaweeds (Seaweed Archetype), while securing dynamic hydrodynamic stability against wave motion.

### Modular Biomimetic 4-Matrix Spectrum

Defines a surface control combination matrix applicable according to regional objectives and environmental conditions.

- **Matrix 1 (Top Clean / Bottom Clean) —** Dual-sided anti-fouling type (high-speed vessel fairways, military markers).
- **Matrix 2 (Top Reef / Bottom Reef) —** Dual-sided bio-induction type (dedicated artificial reefs, marine forest creation zones).
- **Matrix 3 (Top Clean / Bottom Reef) —** Forward hybrid type (main concept of this white paper: navigational buoys, aquaculture infrastructure).
- **Matrix 4 (Top Reef / Bottom Clean) —** Reverse hybrid type (inverted structure: Top encourages seaweed settlement for surface nutrient absorption and photosynthetic purification; Bottom maintains a clean surface to prevent biofouling on underwater precision sensors and sonar).
  - **Application Forms:** Fixed (Buoy) / Floating (Float). Surface combination (Matrix) and application form represent separate axes.
  - **4a Large Floating Entity —** Existing domain such as large semi-submersible artificial islands, closer to ship extensions. Prior cases are summarized in Section 6. Not the primary exploration target of this paper.
  - **4b Small Floating Entity (Seed Boat) —** Buoy-scale small float. If large structures are ship extensions, small ones are akin to "ocean seeds." **Recorded in this white paper solely as a direction for follow-up exploration.** Detailed concepts are in Sections 4.3–4.5, prior literature in Section 6.

### Internal and Mooring Modules (Ecosystem-Coexisting Energy Design)

- **Primary Power (Minimized Photosynthesis Charging Module) —** Applies transparent flexible solar film and small wave energy harvesting on the Top Zone to achieve self-sufficient power for daytime sensors and data logging. The Mushroom microstructure mitigates surface contamination to maintain light transmission and reflection efficiency.
- **Auxiliary Power (Emergency Power System - EPS) —** Reserved backup power operating exclusively during non-charging conditions such as night, low irradiance, or severe weather. Maintained fully charged without discharging during normal operations. Aims for low-capacity, low-toxicity types such as seawater batteries or solid-state batteries to minimize environmental impact in case of marine leakage.
- **Amber Light Mode (Ecosystem Light Pollution Mitigation) —** Top Zone LEDs adopt an Amber mode to protect the night dark environment of the 5-tier lower ecosystem.
  - **Color —** Excludes white and short-wavelength blue light, prioritizing amber wavelengths (Amber, 590–620 nm).
  - **Brightness —** Minimal luminous intensity satisfying IALA minimum visibility distance requirements.
  - **Direction —** Applies a full-cutoff shield to block/minimize downward light spillage, directing light horizontally for surface identification only.
  - **Flashing —** Avoids continuous lighting, adopting intermittent flashing (e.g., 1 flash per 10-second cycle) to guarantee at least 9 seconds of darkness, protecting sleep and biological rhythms of marine life.
- **Bottom Mooring —** Mooring chain connected to seabed anchors.

## 3. Functions and Ecological Structure: Multi-Tiered Vertical Ecosystem

The Bottom Zone of the buoy is conceptualized as a 5-tier vertical ecosystem apartment in sea areas suffering from ocean desertification:

1. **Tier 1 (Base Substrate Layer) —** Sessile organisms such as barnacles (Barnacle Archetype) and mussels settle to form a firm biological outer wall foundation (e.g., barnacles acting as attachment substrate).
2. **Tier 2 (Three-Dimensional Outer Wall Layer) —** Bivalves and oyster colonies grow to form porous 3D structures contributing to water filtration (e.g., oysters/mussels purifying water via filter feeding and providing shelter).
3. **Tier 3 (Dynamic Resident Layer) —** Small benthic organisms inhabit the interstitial voids formed within the structure (e.g., crabs/shrimps forming benthic crustacean food webs).
4. **Tier 4 (Vegetation Environment Layer) —** Seaweed spores (Seaweed Archetype) settle on mooring chains and lower surfaces to perform photosynthesis and oxygen supply (e.g., sea mustard/green laver providing vegetation cover and oxygenation).
5. **Tier 5 (Nursery Layer) —** Forms a juvenile fish shelter around seaweeds and biological communities to avoid predators (e.g., juvenile rockfish/sea bream avoiding apex predators and rearing).

### 4.3 Matrix 4b Expansion Concept: Seed Boat (Small Station-Keeping Floating Entity)

> **Status Notice:** This section asserts no original claims or rights, but proposes a **combination direction** connecting existing research and technologies summarized in Section 6 to a small ecological floating body. All points below are unverified hypotheses without numerical validation.

**Definition.** A buoy-scaled small floating entity. It drifts naturally within a designated zone, storing energy during normal periods and using stored energy only to return when drifting near zone boundaries. It carries pot-type vegetation in the splash zone (above water) while maintaining a clean surface below the waterline (Matrix 4 structure).

**Design Principles**

1. **Small Scale Upper Bound (Energy Budget) —** Because return energy relies on stored charge, physical size is capped by the range returnable within that energy budget. Consequently, water purification effects remain secondary hypotheses.
2. **Zone Keeping —** Uses natural drift (tides, wind, waves) without seabed anchors to trace large trajectories (reciprocating/circular) inside the zone, applying fine motor/steering adjustments only near boundaries using stored energy. Assumes coastal waters with reversing tidal currents. Movement functions as a boundary-return mechanism, not continuous propulsion.
3. **Environmental Forces Considered —** Tidal currents, wind, and wave forces serve as primary inputs. Coriolis deflection due to Earth's rotation is treated as a correction factor for long-duration or wide-area operations. Gravity (universal gravitation) forms the origin of tides and the premise for buoyancy/stability design. The relative weighting of each force per water body type remains a verification task.
4. **Adaptive Control —** Regulates motor, steering angle, ballast, and line drag via feedback to maintain position, aiming to reduce energy consumption by predicting drift using accumulated conditional data. Uses safe-side fixed rules during early data-scarce phases or extreme storms.
5. **Sensors —** Combines GPS, IMU, anemometer, and line tension sensors as core inputs, supplemented by tidal/weather forecasts. Minimizes continuously active sensors, switching to a safe default state (lowered ballast, low-profile stance) upon sensor anomaly. Anti-fouling is required near the waterline sensor surface.
6. **Ballast (Composite) —** As the underwater section is kept clean, natural ballast from biofouling is not expected. Low-toxicity fixed artificial weight (keel) provides baseline stability, excluding toxic heavy metals (lead, cadmium) due to open-water connectivity. Modulating the center of gravity via electronically actuated movable weights is considered. Lowering operates passively under gravity as a failsafe (lowering upon power loss), while raising utilizes stored energy.
7. **Low-Profile Mode (Storm) —** Maintains positive buoyancy keeping vegetation above water during calm periods, but floods lower air chambers during storms to lower posture near the waterline, reducing wind drag and center of mass. Manages minimum freeboard, temporary submergence tolerance of plants, post-storm recovery energy, and radar/visual visibility.
8. **Line Damping —** Line reels utilize a drag mechanism (releasing line above set tension thresholds and retracting when slack) to cushion wave and storm impacts. Low-energy mechanisms (elastomers or wave-energy harvesting) are prioritized for retraction.
9. **Normal vs. Storm Rule Separation —** Under normal operation, weights function strictly without contacting the seabed (distinguishing from fixed artificial islands). Sea anchors or temporary anchor modes are candidates during extreme storms, alongside pre-storm evacuation or retrieval options.
10. **Minimizing Existing Ecosystem Interference —** Restricted strictly to indigenous species, zone-bounded movement, low-toxicity power, and detachable/recoverable plant module structures upon failure.
11. **Vegetation —** Vines, reeds, halophytes, and intertidal seaweeds are candidates without finalization. Tolerance to temporary submergence and salinity fluctuation serves as selection criteria. Air and water purification effects are recorded as unverified hypotheses.
12. **Coordinate System: Absolute for Land, Relative for Water —** Land structures anchor to Earth-fixed absolute coordinates. Water-bound bodies move with water masses; forcing an Earth-fixed point increases energy expenditure, mooring loads, and collision risks. Matrix 4b defines its zone relative to **surrounding water flow, neighboring floats, and reference buoys**, correcting relative deviations. Matrix 3 fixed buoys act as absolute reference points (survival coordinates), while 4b maintains its zone relative to those references. This aligns with "point-keeping vs. zone-keeping" and Lagrangian vs. Eulerian perspectives, though application to 4b remains an unverified hypothesis.
13. **Depth (Z) Axis —** Beyond horizontal x-y positioning, water adds depth as a third axis. In 4b, the depth axis has two aspects: (a) Draft/Freeboard: altering exposed height above water via variable ballast and floodable compartments (low-profile mode); (b) Drogue/Anchor Depth: current velocity and direction vary with depth; spooling line to adjust drogue depth allows selecting water layers with weaker or favorable current vectors. This depth-selection mechanism is recorded as unverified hypothesis H8.

**Operational Modes in Normal vs. Storm Conditions —**
- **Position —** Normal: Natural drift and boundary correction within zone / Storm: Sea anchor, temporary anchor mode, or pre-evacuation.
- **Posture —** Normal: Vegetation positioned above waterline / Storm: Flooded lower compartments maintaining low profile.
- **Ballast —** Normal: Raising/lowering within non-seabed-contact range / Storm: Failsafe lowering and potential anchor mode transition.
- **Control —** Normal: Adaptive feedback and data-driven prediction / Storm: Safe-side fixed rules.
- **Energy —** Normal: Charging and storage priority, minimal sensors active / Storm: Reserved backup energy secured for post-storm return.

**Inferences from Connecting Prior Literature (Inference)**

Within the search scope at the time of authoring, no single publication or patent combining all these elements was identified (see Section 6.3). Therefore, connecting individual prior studies yields the following **inferences** representing logical possibilities rather than literature-proven conclusions:

- **Inference 1 — Marine Applicability of Vegetated Floats:** Floating Treatment Wetlands (FTWs) have been studied using floating mats, emergent plants, and root biofilms for water treatment, alongside studies selecting brackish plant species and growing halophytes/mangroves in floating raft pots over long periods in seawater. → Splash-zone pot vegetation is logically plausible at sea. However, reports show root submergence directly in seawater hindered growth, suggesting that a Clean-below-waterline structure with potted plants may be structurally coherent.
- **Inference 2 — Station-Keeping of Small Floats:** Studies exist on energy-efficient station-keeping for small autonomous surface vessels, low-power steering using ocean currents, and variable-drag drifters. → Using drift to remain within a zone has technical precedent.
- **Inference 3 — Data-Driven Improvement:** Adaptive planners incorporating predicted ocean currents, online learning for optimal station-keeping, reinforcement learning for energy-saving navigation, and drift velocity prediction patents exist. → Utilizing accumulated data to predict drift and save energy is an extension of prior art.
- **Inference 4 — Storm Low-Profile Posture:** Patents exist for large offshore platforms submerging via variable ballast during storms and re-floating via compressed air, as well as wave energy converters submerging for storm survival. → Applying the same principle scaled down to a small vegetated float represents a conceptual adaptation.
- **Inference 5 — Inevitability of Upper Scale Limit:** Small-reservoir model estimations indicated that insufficient plant surface coverage yielded negligible purification, estimating ~40% coverage requirement under peak influx scenarios. Meanwhile, return energy relies on stored capacity, capping physical scale. → Matrix 4b should be evaluated on small scale, minimal interference, and return capability rather than purification performance; purification remains a secondary hypothesis.

**Verifiable Hypotheses Derived from Inferences (Unverified)**

- H1: There exist plant species combinations in splash-zone pots capable of enduring local salinity fluctuations and temporary storm submergence.
- H2: In coastal waters with reversing tides, steering corrections alone can maintain zone trajectories within stored energy budgets.
- H3: Predictive control based on accumulated data reduces correction energy compared to rule-based feedback (evaluated including initial data-scarce phases).
- H4: Low-profile posture via lower compartment flooding reduces wind drag and drift while satisfying minimum freeboard and stability limits.
- H5: Normal non-seabed-contact and clean underwater structures reduce existing ecosystem interference compared to fixed structures.
- H6: In water bodies with geomorphically induced circulation (e.g., narrow-mouthed bays), utilizing natural circulation vectors reduces correction energy required for zone-keeping.
- H7: Defining zones relative to water, neighbors, and reference buoys instead of Earth-fixed absolute coordinates reduces correction energy and mooring loads (absolute position reporting and fairway compliance are separately secured via reference buoys and GPS).
- H8: Adjusting drogue/anchor depth via line length to select favorable current layers reduces return energy compared to surface steering alone (dynamic cable loads and fishing gear entanglement risks require separate evaluation).

### 4.4 Purpose and Spread of Small-Scale-First Development

- **Purpose of Small-Scale-First Development —** Small structures aim to accumulate technical experience rather than maximize immediate purification efficiency. Testing low-cost, technology-dense small units builds operational data on sensors, control, ballasting, and low-profile survival, establishing a stepwise path toward medium and large structures. Control algorithms and data models exhibit high cross-scale portability, though structural mechanics and stability require re-verification per scale. Functions should be added incrementally. Increased failure points and saltwater corrosion burdens on compact bodies must also be managed.
- **Entry Barriers and Diffusion —** Small scale lowers fabrication and testing costs, facilitating participation by small research teams in alignment with open sharing (CC BY 4.0). However, to prevent risks of marine littering, invasive species introduction, and fairway interference, testing permits, scale limits, clear visual/radar identification, and explicit retrieval responsibilities are strongly recommended.

### 4.5 Phased Test Pathways and Water Body Types

> **Drafting Principle:** To avoid singling out specific nations or geographic locations, water bodies in this section are described strictly by **geomorphological and hydrodynamics types**. Candidate site selection occurs separately during test planning through managing authority approvals and site surveys. Type-specific characteristics below are hypotheses grounded in general oceanographic principles, including unverified elements.

**Phased Pathway (Candidates):** Initial testing of wind/wave response and control in enclosed freshwater, followed by salinity/tidal testing in diked brackish/seawater lakes. Next, verifying circulation utilization and control in geomorphically favorable waters (narrow bays, gateway channels, island-shielded waters), culminating in storm and boundary-return testing in exposed coastal waters. Hypersaline or geologically active zones are evaluated via literature comparison rather than direct deployment.

- **① Enclosed Freshwater Lakes —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** No tides; wind and wave driven.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Verification of wind/wave response, zone-keeping control, ballasting, low-profile stance, and line damping.
  - **Validation Limits:** Cannot verify tidal utilization (H2), salinity tolerance (H1), or seawater corrosion.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Invasive species impacts in closed systems; water supply protection restrictions.
- **② Estuarine / Seawater-Circulating Lakes Separated by Dikes —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Controlled seawater exchange via sluice gates; brackish to saline environment.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Salinity and tidal condition testing (H1, H2); gate current verification.
  - **Validation Limits:** Cannot verify open-sea wave or typhoon conditions.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Strong suction/currents near gates; water quality stratification (potential deep hypoxia); operational policy changes.
- **③ Lagoons / Brackish Lakes —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Low salinity to brackish; minimal tidal amplitude.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Low-salinity vegetation tolerance; high-latitude freezing tests.
  - **Validation Limits:** Cannot verify tidal utilization or open-sea conditions.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** High conservation value in many lagoons; comparative literature review prioritized over direct testing.
- **④ Narrow-Mouthed Bays / Inner Bays —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Potential for geomorphically induced circulation and eddies.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Zone-keeping using natural circulation (H6); ease of recovery.
  - **Validation Limits:** Limited open-sea wave condition testing.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Rapid tidal currents at narrow entrances; temporal circulation shifts; marine debris accumulation; navigation channel interference.
- **⑤ Designated Gateway Water Bodies (Gates, Locks, Channels) —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Formed inflow/outflow timing and directional jet vectors.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Adaptive control validation under predictable forcing inputs.
  - **Validation Limits:** Cannot cover full natural variability.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Suction/jet hazards; increased return energy during unidirectional flow; lock schedule changes.
- **⑥ Island-Shielded Semi-Enclosed Waters —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Attenuated waves; natural boundaries; inter-island channel currents.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Initial stability and baseline zone-keeping control validation.
  - **Validation Limits:** Limited storm and open-sea condition testing.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Strong channel currents; overlap with aquaculture farms, fairways, or protected areas.
- **⑦ Exposed Coastal Waters —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Direct exposure to open-sea waves and full tidal forcing.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Final validation of storm survival, low-profile mode, and return limits.
  - **Validation Limits:** None.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Vessel collision hazards, typhoons, recovery difficulty.
- **⑧ Hypersaline / Geologically Active Zones —**
  - **Hydrodynamic Characteristics:** Extreme salinity; rapid shifts in temperature, gas, or geomorphology.
  - **Testing Purpose:** Material and vegetation limit testing (comparative literature review prioritized).
  - **Validation Limits:** None.
  - **Risks & Cautions:** Geomorphological instability making direct testing unsuitable.

**Comparative Water Body Classification (for Literature Review):** Semi-enclosed tideless brackish seas (low salinity/freezing comparison), high-latitude archipelago channels (shielding/strong current comparison), tropical cyclone prone seas (extreme storm comparison), and pocket harbors (Type ④ comparison) are evaluated via literature comparison prior to physical field tests.

## 5. Future Value and Implementation Challenges: Recording Feasibility

### Technical Challenges and Interpretive Concepts

- Securing cost-effective mass production processes for top-zone mushroom microstructures.
- Reviewing legal and technical alignment with international navigation standards (IALA).
- **Reinterpretation of Buoyancy and Weight Balance —** Reinterpreting lower biological attachment not as a simple weight liability, but as "natural lower ballast (Self-Ballasting) and hydrodynamic damping." Weight addition below lowers the center of gravity, enhancing metacenter height (GM) and righting moments against wind roll, while seaweed drag attenuates dynamic wave tossing. However, this reinterpretation remains a conceptual proposal prior to empirical validation; initial buoyancy guidelines aim to establish a guideline limiting maximum anticipated bottom growth weight to within 30% of total reserve buoyancy to prevent sinking.
- **Ecosystem Light Pollution Mitigation Design —** Amber mode aims for minimal luminous intensity satisfying IALA minimum visibility requirements and 1 flash per 10-second cycle, mandating full-cutoff shielding to eliminate downward light spillage.

### 4b (Seed Boat) Follow-Up Verification Tasks

Items with existing research should be **re-verified under small-scale floating conditions across open-sea wave, salinity, and storm environments**.

- Plant species selection, temporary submergence/salinity tolerance, root placement vs. Matrix positions (plants with submerged roots may void Clean-below-waterline status).
- Wind drag and center-of-mass impact of splash-zone plants; ease of storm replacement/re-planting.
- Return capability limits (stored energy vs. current/wind speeds); applicability in non-reversing tidal seas.
- Power consumption and biofouling across sensor types; safe default states during sensor faults; loss of forecast signal responses.
- Variable ballast operating depth, cable dynamic loads, fishing gear entanglement, and saltwater durability of line damping.
- Marine pollution risks of ballast/structural materials (excluding lead/cadmium, leaching under corrosion/wear, lost unit recovery): Open-sea connectivity means local contamination spreads, requiring material safety and recovery planning at early design phases.
- Low-profile mode freeboard limits, free surface effects, reserve buoyancy thresholds, post-storm return energy, and radar/visual visibility.
- Anchoring limits (drag/slippage), benthic sensitivity, and pre-storm evacuation/retrieval operations.
- Magnetic anchor method (Research task, unverified idea): Exploring temporary storm mooring using electro-permanent magnets attaching to pre-installed magnetic seabed pads. Key verifications include seabed contact of pre-installed pads (aligning with normal non-contact principles), magnetic force reduction from gaps/sediments/fouling, power loss failsafe release, and comparisons with suction anchors. Natural seabeds lack ferromagnetic material, restricting use to pre-installed sites. Furthermore, magnet/pad materials (rare-earth corrosion/plating, iron/steel oxidation products) could act as marine contamination sources; material leaching, corrosion, and recovery upon damage/loss must be evaluated conservatively. Arbitrary installation of magnetic materials or pads in marine environments risks heavy metal pollution, benthic habitat alteration, and magnetic interference with marine fauna and navigation sensors. Consequently, deployment must not proceed without regulatory permits and environmental impact reviews, accompanied by records of position, material, quantity, and decommissioning/retrieval plans.
- Restriction to indigenous species, prevention of invasive species spread, recovery structures upon damage, and zone/fairway alignment.
- Measurability of air/water purification effects at small scales.
- Verification by test water body type: Regulatory permits, suction/current hazards near sluice gates, fairway/fishery overlaps, plant tolerance to temperature/salinity shifts.
- Defining limits for extrapolating closed-freshwater test results to ocean conditions (salinity, waves, tides).

### Future Value as a White Paper

- This white paper aims to record the possibility of a paradigm shift fusing eco-friendly buoys, artificial reefs, aquaculture data infrastructure, and marine ecosystem restoration technologies, rather than immediate commercial product manufacturing.
- **Expansion Possibilities —** As technology advances, applications to extreme environments or other domains may open up, though this white paper leaves them as open follow-up tasks without addressing them directly.
- It is hoped that this document serves as a foundational conceptual anchor for future researchers investigating eco-friendly marine materials or integrated marine ecosystem monitoring systems.

Designed for research/residence rather than ecological restoration; represents a large mobile artificial island case.

**Matrix 4b (Small Floating Entity, Seed Boat) Related — Element-wise Prior Research/Technology**

*a. Floating Vegetation*
- Floating Treatment Wetland (FTW) Reviews: Reviews cover purification principles and design elements (vegetation cover, species, depth, seasonal variations) via floating mats, emergent plants, and root biofilms (e.g., Applied Sciences 2025 review on FTWs in nutrient/heavy metal bioremediation, Kumar et al.).
- Brackish Applications: Study evaluating nutrient uptake of two halophytes (*Spartina alterniflora*, *Juncus roemerianus*) under varying salinities (0.5, 5.0, 18 g/L) in brackish FTWs (Water, Air, & Soil Pollution 2025).
- Marine Floating Cultivation: US20050044788A1 (Method for growing terrestrial plants on floating platforms in seawater of varying salinity; published, presumed abandoned; verification recommended). Radulovich et al. (Aquaculture Reports, 2017) grew 2 mangrove and 2 halophyte herb species in floating raft pots in tropical coastal waters for 733 days on seawater alone (including a 504-day rainless period); direct root submergence in initial trials caused nutrient deficiency and poor growth.
- Offshore Dual-Layer Floating Solar Farm (Wu et al., Chemical Engineering Journal 473, 145452, 2023): Cultivated broccoli, lettuce, and pak choi above sea level using freshwater produced by solar evaporation, reporting 80% survival in long-term field trials.
- Floating Salt Marsh: Project where universities and water utility companies collaborate to test coastal restoration by placing salt marsh vegetation on floating pontoons (reported April 2026).
- Domestic Artificial Floating Islands: KR20170003882A (Published 2017-01-10, registered as KR101711351B1; publication verified, registration status needs verification): Floating artificial island for planting aquatic vegetation installed in lakes, reservoirs, dams, and wetlands. Claim 1 combines net-type support seats, floating bodies with planting holes, sunlight-blocking sheets shielding the body from sunlight, non-woven reinforcement materials, support frames, and fixing wires. Stated purposes include fish spawning grounds, water purification, aquatic ecosystem restoration, waterfront space creation, weed suppression, plants shading sunlight to reduce turbidity and algal blooms, wave attenuation, and durability enhancement. Claim 9 includes tension adjusting weight members on connecting ropes with fixed anchors to adjust line tension against water level fluctuations (designed for freshwater, marine application unstated). Lee et al. (1999) planted *Typha angustifolia*, *Zizania latifolia*, and *Phragmites australis* on floating islands; all survived, with reed demonstrating optimal shoot-root balance. Sim et al. (2001) reported reeds grew well at 50 cm water depth, whereas water dropwort died off during winter (water dropwort test absent in Lee 1999, present in Sim 2001). Park et al. (2000) estimated ~40% floating cover required under peak influx concentrations (0.220 mg-TP/L) in small reservoirs (model-based estimate, non-generalizable value; full text verified).

*b. Station-Keeping / Drift Utilization*
- Oh & Kim, J. Mar. Sci. Technol. 25(1), 13–25 (2020, DOI 10.1007/s00773-019-00628-1): Comparison of station-keeping strategies (fixed point vs. designated zone) for long-endurance autonomous surface vessels.
- Autonomous Seaweed Farm (arXiv 2307.01916, 2023): Unmoored autonomous farm drifting with ocean currents, utilizing low thrust (~0.1 m/s) to steer along currents and avoid grounding, collisions, or nutrient-poor zones.
- Variable-Drag Drifter: US9676455B2 (Registered 2017, listed as expired; verification recommended). Claim 1 discloses a partially submerged hull alternating between current-following (high frontal area) and transit (low frontal area) states via variable frontal area mechanisms, where high frontal area is at least 3 times the low state. Steerable drifter dynamics are also studied.
- Small Solar-Charged Drifter: STRIDR (AGU OSM 2020, low-cost, compact, charged by 4 solar panels, equipped with GPS and 10-DOF IMU for DARPA Ocean of Things). Wave Glider (Liquid Robotics) utilizes solar-charged 0.9–6.8 kWh battery packs and auxiliary vector thrusters based on manufacturer data (verification recommended: manufacturer datasheet link unverified; citing official datasheet instead of Wikipedia recommended).
- Virtual Mooring: Glider station-keeping reflecting predictive ocean circulation models (Clark et al., IEEE J. Oceanic Eng. 45(2), 371–384, April 2020, DOI 10.1109/JOE.2018.2886092).
- Station-Keeping General: KR20110116385A describes an offshore energy/desalination hybrid system mentioning GPS auxiliary propulsion station-keeping, but Claim 1 covers broad hybrid systems and was rejected/closed. Acts as specification disclosure rather than core station-keeping prior art. PNNL Report (PNNL-31719, 2021, Powering the Blue Economy: A Survey of Station-Keeping Methods for Mooringless Platforms) surveyed unmoored station-keeping methods.
- Small Platform Behavior: J. Mar. Sci. Eng. 11, 506 (2023) proposes an uncrewed auto-leveling ocean drifting platform (0.7 m diameter prototype), noting that wave forces significantly impact small platforms (~2 m diameter).

*c. Data / Adaptive Control*
- Walters et al. (arXiv 1710.10511, 2017): Online approximation of optimal vessel station-keeping in currents via concurrent learning-based identification without prior drift dynamics.
- Gadhvi & Manjanna (arXiv 2512.06912, 2025): Soft Actor-Critic reinforcement learning navigation using local flow velocity measurements in vortex flow fields, reporting 30–50% energy savings over state-of-the-art methods.
- Drift Velocity Prediction Patent: Deep learning drift prediction using wave height, wind, and current data is identified in patents (e.g., CN114580309A; claims unverified).

*d. Storm Response / Ballast*
- Variable ballast submergence platforms with compressed air re-buoyancy and sea anchors: US9764804B1 (2017, parent) and US10399648B1 (2019, continuation-in-part) belong to the same patent family (listed as active; verification recommended). Dependent claims include variable ballast tanks, compressed air re-buoyancy, and sea anchors for storm survival.
- Submerging wave energy converter patent US8308449B2 (2012, presumed expired; verification recommended): Variable buoyancy control via chamber flooding/expulsion for storm submergence survival.
- Sea anchors, drogues, reel drag, and constant-tension winches represent standard naval architecture technologies (Chapman Piloting & Seamanship, USCG Auxiliary Navigation Manual, ISO 17357).

*f. Relative Coordinates (Water / Neighbor Reference)*
- Observing water masses in a Lagrangian frame (moving with water) represents standard oceanographic methodology (e.g., FlocDrifter, J. Atmos. Oceanic Technol. 32(3), 547–561, 2015; Stewart's Physical Oceanography textbook Section 10.8 defines Lagrangian measurement as following water particles versus Eulerian fixed-point measurement).
- Formation control research exists for multiple surface vessels maintaining relative positions (e.g., streamerless formation control for marine seismic surveys, arXiv 2308.03699; NPS visual marker USV swarm coordination).
- Literature bibliographies are verified. Defining zones via relative coordinates (water, neighbors, reference buoys) to reduce correction energy in small vegetated floats remains an unverified hypothesis (H7).

*g. Depth (Z) Axis*
- Studies evaluating ocean drifters with varying drogue depths to observe depth-dependent flow differences are extensive (e.g., Niiler et al., Deep Sea Res. I 42(11), 1995, 15m drogue current-following performance; Rypina et al., 1–50m multi-layer drifter trials in W. Mediterranean, AGU OSM 2020).
- 3D station-keeping/steering exploiting depth-varying currents: Virtual glider mooring (Clark et al., supra); streamline-based glider control in 3D environments (with a 2.5D assumption ignoring vertical motion; arXiv 2005.03834).
- Depth regulation in aquaculture structures: US11533861B2 (2022) and US12185679B2 (2025) belong to the same family (where the applicant differs due to assignment) and are both listed as active (verification recommended). Claim 1 covers autonomous aquaculture structure control adjusting depth and steering against currents to optimize biological growth environments (e.g., nocturnal deep nutrient absorption, diurnal surface photosynthesis). arXiv 2307.01916 represents related research in this domain.
- Literature bibliographies are verified. Using line length to adjust drogue depth for zone return in small vegetated floats remains an unverified hypothesis (H8).

*e. Test Water Body Types (Geomorphological Description)*
- Reports document sluice gate operations managing seawater exchange and salinity shifts in diked artificial lakes (PLoS ONE 13(12):e0209403, 2018; Int. J. Sediment Res. 35(2), 146–156, 2020; Appl. Water Sci. 2026), serving as references for Type ② test conditions. Salinity variation amounts differ depending on operating methods.
- Inland lakes created by sedimentation, dikes, or tectonic shifts that were formerly part of the sea and later became disconnected and freshened are classified as lagoons or relict lakes in encyclopedia literature (for Type ④/⑧ classification).
- Tidal exchange circulation in narrow bays, current attenuation behind islands, and flow jetting at restricted channels are described in standard oceanographic literature (Emery & Thomson, Fischer et al., Stewart). Applicability to 4b test sites represents general principle application requiring site-specific verification.
